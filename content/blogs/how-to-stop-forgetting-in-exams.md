You read a topic carefully. You understood it. You even made notes. Then, a month later in the exam hall, you see a question on that same topic and the answer just won't come. You *know* you read it, but it's gone.

This happens to almost every student. It does **not** mean your memory is weak. It only means the topic was read once and never revised. The fix is **revision at the right time**, and during my preparation for Punjab Police SI I used a simple method for it: the **1-7-30 rule.**

> **The whole rule in one line:** Study a topic today. Revise it tomorrow, again after 7 days, and again after 30 days.

## Why we forget what we study

Your brain treats anything new as temporary. If you don't use it again, the brain slowly clears it out to make room for other things. Over 100 years ago, the psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus studied this and called it the **forgetting curve**: we forget fastest in the first few days after learning, and then more slowly.

Think of it like a path across a grassy field:

- Walk across once and you leave a faint trail. In a few days the grass grows back and the trail is gone.
- Walk the same way again the next day, then a week later, then a month later, and it becomes a clear path that stays for a long time.

**Revision is walking the same path again.** Each time you revise, the memory gets stronger and lasts longer than before. That is why the gaps in this rule get longer: 1 day, then 7 days, then 30 days.

## The 1-7-30 rule, step by step

### Day 0: Study the topic properly

Read the topic and understand it. While reading, make **short notes**: key points, dates, names, article numbers, anything likely to be asked. Keep them short, because these notes are what you will revise from later.

### Day 1: First revision (the next day)

This is the most important revision, because memory drops fastest in the first day or two. Spend 10, 15, 20 minutes or up to half an hour, depending on how big the topic is. Do it at the start of your day, before you study anything new.

### Day 7: Second revision (one week later)

By now part of the topic has faded. Revising it brings it back, and this time it sticks for much longer. It usually takes less time than the first revision.

### Day 30: Third revision (one month later)

This one takes just a few minutes, because the topic is now almost fixed in your memory. After this, mock tests and a quick look just before the exam are enough to keep it fresh.

So every topic is **studied once and revised three times.** Those three revisions together often take less than an hour, and they save a topic you would otherwise have lost.

## An example: one topic over one month

Say you study Fundamental Rights on 1 December. Count the days from the day you studied it:

| Date | What to do | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 December | Study the topic and make short notes | About 2 hours |
| 2 December (+1 day) | First revision | 20 minutes |
| 8 December (+7 days) | Second revision | 15 minutes |
| 31 December (+30 days) | Third revision | 10 minutes |

Two hours of study plus 45 minutes of revision, and the topic stays with you until exam day.

## "But I study a new topic every day. Won't revisions pile up?"

No. On any single day, your revision list has at most three topics:

1. **Yesterday's topic** (its 1-day revision)
2. **The topic from 7 days ago** (its 7-day revision)
3. **The topic from 30 days ago** (its 30-day revision)

Plus the **new topic** you study that day.

For example, on 31 December you would study a new topic and revise the topics from 30 December, 24 December and 1 December. That is usually 30 to 60 minutes of revision in total. The rest of the day is still for new topics.

## How to revise: this matters as much as when

Most students revise by simply reading the notes again. Rereading feels easy and gives a false feeling of "I know this already". A better way:

1. **Close the book.** Ask yourself: what do I remember about this topic?
2. **Write down the key points** from memory on rough paper. Spend 2-3 minutes on it.
3. **Open your notes and check.** Mark what you missed or got wrong.
4. **Spend most of your revision time on the points you missed.**

Trying to remember first, and then checking, is what makes memory strong. If you can, also solve a few MCQs on the same topic in our [practice section](practice.html).

## Keep a simple revision tracker

Without a record, you will lose track of which topic is due when. Keep a small notebook with one line per topic:

| Topic | Studied | 1-day | 7-day | 30-day |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fundamental Rights | 1 Dec | 2 Dec ✓ | 8 Dec ✓ | 31 Dec |
| Rivers of Punjab | 2 Dec | 3 Dec ✓ | 9 Dec | 1 Jan |

Write all the revision dates on the day you study the topic, and tick each one when it is done. A calendar or a phone reminder works just as well, as long as you use it every day.

## How to fit it into your daily routine

- **Start your first study shift each day with revision.** First yesterday's topic, then any 7-day or 30-day topics that are due.
- **Check the tracker at night.** While planning the next day, see which topics are due for revision tomorrow.
- **Use one day a week to catch up.** If any revision was missed during the week, finish it on that day.

## What if you miss a revision?

Don't panic, and don't skip it. Just do it the next day you can, and count the next gap from the day you actually revised. One late revision is far better than no revision.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **"I'll revise everything at the end."** By the end, hundreds of topics have already faded and there is no time to relearn them.
- **Revising by rereading the whole chapter.** It takes too long, so it gets skipped. Revise from your short notes.
- **Not keeping a record.** Without a tracker, revisions get missed without you noticing.
- **Doing the first revision too late.** If you first revise a week later, most of the topic is already gone. Day 1 matters most.

## Quick summary

- Forgetting is normal. Revision at the right time fixes it.
- Revise every topic after **1 day, 7 days and 30 days.**
- Test yourself first, then check your notes.
- Keep a tracker so no revision is missed.
- Missed a revision? Do it the next day and carry on.

Start today: pick the topic you studied yesterday and revise it before anything else.
