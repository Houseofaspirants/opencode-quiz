Reasoning and Quant: Speed, Accuracy & MCQ Drills

Speed in Reasoning and Quant is a by-product of clean methods and short drills — not talent. Here is how to build both without burning out.

The short answer

Speed in reasoning and quant comes from one method per question type, drilled until it is automatic — not from rushing. Fix accuracy first, because accuracy is what converts into marks, then let speed follow the method.

Key takeaways

  1. One method per question type, drilled until it is automatic — speed comes from recognising the pattern, not from reading faster.
  2. Accuracy before speed. Accuracy converts into marks; speed without accuracy converts into negative marks and regret.
  3. Short daily drills with a timer beat long weekly sessions, because calculation speed is a reflex that decays overnight.
  4. Underline what the question actually asks — units, negatives and the word NOT — to remove most avoidable errors.
  5. Understand the common formulas, then memorise them through use, so a re-derivable formula backs you up under pressure.

Two sections decide most quantitative papers: Reasoning and Mathematics. They are also the two where aspirants improve fastest — because unlike fact-heavy subjects, performance here is a skill you can see improve week by week. The system below is deliberately simple: get accurate first, get fast second, and let short daily drills do the heavy lifting.

Accuracy first, speed second

Speed built on mistakes is just fast guessing. Hold yourself to roughly nine in ten correct in untimed practice before you start racing the clock. When you get something wrong, resist the urge to move on: label the error — silly calculation, misunderstood question, or a method you never actually knew. Two weeks of honest labelling usually reveals that most losses come from the first and third buckets, both of which shrink with practice, not with new theory.

One clean method per topic

Collecting tricks is the enemy of fluency. For each topic family, learn one standard method until it is automatic, then add shortcuts only when they clearly beat it:

  • • Reasoning — series, analogies, coding-decoding, blood relations, direction sense, syllogisms, order and ranking, puzzles and seating arrangements.
  • • Quantitative Aptitude — simplification, percentages, profit and loss, simple/compound interest, ratios, time-speed-distance, work, averages and data interpretation.

Work topic by topic: one week per family, five to ten questions a day inside that family, until accuracy stabilises — then move on. The topic-wise quizzes on each subject page are built exactly for this loop.

Drill daily, short and timed

A fifteen-minute daily sprint beats a three-hour Sunday marathon. Use the same shape every day: five untimed questions to warm up, ten timed questions at a pace slightly harder than comfortable, then review every miss. The Daily Quiz keeps the rotation fresh, and when you want pure volume on one family, build a focused set from the Mock Test Builder with a tight time limit.

Keep two pages: formulas and errors

Page one holds every formula, ratio table and mental-maths anchor you rely on — tables up to 25, squares up to 30, common fraction/percentage equivalents, and the two or three time-saving identities you actually use. Page two is your error log, fed from Bookmarks: every question you flubbed, written up in one line. Re-read both pages weekly. By exam month those two sheets, not a fresh book, are what you revise.

A weekly rhythm that compounds

  • • Mon–Sat: one Reasoning family and one Quant family per day, timed sprint included.
  • • Sunday: mixed set of everything studied so far, then re-solve the error log.
  • • Every two weeks: a full-length mock test to measure speed under real pressure.

Frequently asked questions

Which reasoning topic should I start with?

Start with the topic you can already half-solve. Pattern recognition builds fastest where the logic is already familiar, so series, coding-decoding and direction questions usually give the quickest early gains.

How do I get faster at calculation?

Drill short daily arithmetic sets with a timer instead of long weekly ones. Learn the two or three mental shortcuts you keep needing, and stop writing steps you can hold in your head.

Should I memorise formulas or understand them?

Understand the ones that appear often, then memorise them through use. A formula you can re-derive in five seconds is safer under exam pressure than one you can only recite.

How do I stop making silly mistakes?

Read the question twice and underline what it actually asks — units, negatives and the word NOT. Most lost marks are misread questions, not failed concepts, and the fix is procedural.
